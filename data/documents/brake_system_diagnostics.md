# Brake System Diagnostics Guide

## System Overview
A typical hydraulic disc/drum brake system consists of the brake pedal,
master cylinder, brake booster, brake lines, calipers or wheel cylinders,
rotors or drums, pads or shoes, and (on modern vehicles) an ABS module
with wheel speed sensors. Pressing the brake pedal creates friction
between the brake pads or shoes and the rotor or drum, which slows the
vehicle down.

## Common Symptoms and Causes

### Squealing or Squeaking When Braking
A high-pitched squeal is often a harmless, built-in warning: most brake
pads have a mechanical wear indicator tab that is designed to contact the
rotor and make noise specifically to tell the driver it's time for new
pads. Squealing can also come from glazed pads, dust/debris between the
pad and rotor, a rotor surface that hasn't fully "mated" to new pads yet,
or a thin film of surface rust that forms overnight and wears off after
the first few stops.
**Action:** Measure remaining pad thickness; replace pads if worn down to
the indicators. If squealing continues for more than a couple of days
despite normal pad thickness, have the system inspected.

### Grinding or Scraping Noise When Braking
A grinding noise indicates metal-on-metal contact — the pads have worn
through completely — while a lighter scraping sound can also mean a bent
backing plate or a stuck pebble rubbing on a moving brake part.
**Action:** Inspect immediately — grinding indicates advanced wear and
likely rotor damage; replace pads and resurface or replace rotors as
needed. Continued driving with a grinding noise causes rotor damage that
becomes more expensive to fix.

### Soft or Spongy Brake Pedal
A spongy or inconsistent pedal — where the pressure needed to engage the
brakes changes unpredictably — usually points to air trapped in the
hydraulic lines, which compresses instead of transmitting pressure the
way brake fluid should. Other causes include a low fluid level, a worn
master cylinder, or a leaking wheel cylinder/caliper. Spongy brakes can
also contribute to brake fade after prolonged or heavy braking, since
trapped air lets the fluid heat up more and lose effectiveness. A
dashboard brake warning light illuminating alongside a soft pedal is a
sign to have the system inspected promptly.
**Action:** Check the fluid level and condition first (a level well below
"full," or fluid that looks dark/cloudy instead of clear-to-amber, points
to a leak or overdue fluid service). Inspect for leaks at each wheel and
along the brake lines. Bleed the brake system starting from the wheel
farthest from the master cylinder.

### Vehicle Pulls to One Side When Braking
**Causes:** A stuck caliper slide pin, uneven pad wear, a contaminated
pad or rotor surface (oil/grease), or a collapsed brake hose restricting
flow to one wheel.
**Action:** Inspect caliper slide pins for corrosion; check for a
collapsed hose by comparing pressure release at each wheel.

### Pulsation or Vibration in the Brake Pedal
**Causes:** Warped or out-of-round rotors, usually from excessive heat or
overtightened lug nuts.
**Action:** Measure rotor thickness variation and runout with a dial
indicator; resurface within spec or replace.

### ABS Warning Light On
**Causes:** A faulty wheel speed sensor, a damaged tone ring, a wiring
fault, or low brake fluid triggering a related warning.
**Action:** Scan the ABS module for codes; common code C0035/C0040-series
codes point to a specific wheel's speed sensor circuit.

## Brake Pad Wear Inspection
- New pad thickness is typically 10–12 mm; most pads are expected to last
  roughly 25,000–60,000 miles, though this varies with driving style and
  conditions.
- Replace when friction material reaches the wear indicator or about 3 mm
  or less.
- Uneven wear (inner vs. outer pad) usually indicates a sticking caliper
  slide pin or piston.
- Always replace pads in axle pairs, never individually.

## Brake Fluid Service
- Brake fluid (typically DOT 3 or DOT 4) is hygroscopic and absorbs
  moisture over time, which lowers its boiling point and reduces braking
  performance.
- General guidance suggests a fluid flush roughly every 2 years or
  25,000–30,000 miles, though the owner's manual is the authority for a
  specific vehicle.
- Dark or cloudy fluid indicates contamination and should be flushed.

## Rotor Service Specifications (General Guidance)
- Minimum thickness is stamped on the rotor hub or listed in the service
  manual; never machine below this spec.
- Maximum allowable runout is typically 0.05–0.08 mm; exceeding it causes
  pedal pulsation.
